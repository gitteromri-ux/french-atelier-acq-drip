## Landing page

# French words to use around a Christmas table

Learn French through a Lyon Christmas custom with French Atelier. Les papillotes give you a simple situation to talk about: someone unwraps a chocolate, finds a message and reads it aloud. Here is how to understand the custom and join the conversation.

## A sweet with a local connection

Papillotes are especially linked to Lyon and the surrounding region. Chocolate is the familiar filling, although some contain pâte de fruits, a fruit confection. The festive wrapper and printed message are what make the sweet recognisable. This is a regional tradition, not a rule for every French Christmas table.

## Unwrap, read, share

Imagine a small bowl passed around after a meal. Someone opens the metallic paper and finds a quotation; someone else reads a joke. A riddle can give the whole table something to guess before anyone reveals the answer. You do not need a long conversation to join in: simply reading the little note aloud is part of the pleasure.

## A story to treat as a legend

One popular Lyon legend connects the papillote to a young confectioner who wrapped sweets in love notes. It is a charming explanation, but it should be enjoyed as a legend rather than treated as a proven account of the sweet’s invention.

## Practise a question and an answer

Ask, « Qu’est-ce qui est écrit ? » That means, “What does it say?” If the message is a riddle, answer, « C’est une devinette. » That means, “It’s a riddle.” Say both sentences aloud, then try the answer without looking at the English. Un petit mot names the message; un emballage names the wrapper. You can now ask about the note and describe what you find.

# Learn to describe Lyon’s lights in French

Use Lyon’s Festival of Lights to practise French with French Atelier. Learn what the candles mean, how the modern festival differs from the older tradition, and how to turn a few useful words into sentences about the city.

## The tradition that began in 1852

The celebration’s roots lie in the inauguration of a statue of the Virgin Mary on Fourvière hill. In 1852, residents placed candles in their windows in her honour. These small lights became a lasting local tradition. Today’s light-art festival developed much later. The candles belong to an older community custom that continues alongside it.

## What is a lumignon?

Un lumignon is a small candle, traditionally set in a glass holder on a windowsill. Together, rows of these lights make residents’ homes part of the celebration. This is a simple, personal contribution alongside the larger public displays. It helps explain why the festival is more than a spectacle viewed from the pavement.

## Historic buildings, contemporary art

In public squares, artists use colour, movement and projected images to change how familiar architecture appears. At Place des Terreaux, the façades around the square can become the setting for large light works. Installations change between editions, so a photograph records a particular artistic interpretation rather than a permanent decoration.

## Practise describing what you see

La lumière means light; la ville means the city; illuminer means to illuminate. Read « La ville s’illumine » aloud: “The city lights up.” Now try « Je vois un lumignon »: “I see a small candle.” Je vois means “I see”, so it helps you name something in front of you. Look at the photograph, choose one of these sentences and say it without reading the English.

# French greetings and words for Saint Nicolas

Learn how to greet someone and describe a celebration with French Atelier. Saint Nicolas traditions in Lorraine and Alsace give these French words a context: a procession, gifts and a bishop known for generosity. This is a regional custom, not a celebration shared identically across France.

## The person behind the celebration

Saint Nicholas was a Christian bishop of Myra, in what is now Turkey. He became known through stories of generosity and protection, especially towards children. Those stories helped inspire gift-giving customs. In French celebrations, his bishop’s clothing recalls that religious origin: the tall hat is a mitre, and the curved pastoral staff is a crosse.

## A tradition with regional character

Nancy, in Lorraine, is a well-known centre of the festivities. Processions bring Saint Nicolas into the streets, with families watching and children waiting for treats. Customs differ between towns and households. Sweets, small gifts and pain d’épices, or spiced gingerbread, are among the familiar pleasures. Gingerbread figures shaped like the saint make an especially recognisable detail.

## Who is le Père Fouettard?

In some local traditions, Saint Nicolas is accompanied by le Père Fouettard. He is the stern character who contrasts with the generous saint and reminds children about good behaviour. Their contrasting roles are part of the local folklore. Saint Nicolas remains the kindly figure children look forward to meeting, especially when he arrives with treats.

## Practise a greeting and a sentence

Say « Bonne fête de Saint-Nicolas ! » aloud: “Happy Saint Nicholas Day!” Then try « Je regarde le défilé »: “I’m watching the parade.” Un défilé means “a parade”; le défilé means “the parade”. Les friandises means sweets, and un cadeau means a gift. Cover the English meanings and see which words you remember. You now have a greeting to offer and a sentence to describe what you are doing.

# What to say in French at New Year

Practise French you can use in a greeting, a message or a toast with French Atelier. Learn which expression fits each situation, and understand le réveillon du Nouvel An: a late festive meal whose name is connected to réveiller, “to wake up”.

## Two celebrations called réveillon

French uses réveillon for both the Christmas Eve celebration, le réveillon de Noël, and the New Year’s Eve celebration, le réveillon du Nouvel An. The rest of the phrase tells you which one is meant. La Saint-Sylvestre is another name for New Year’s Eve, while réveillon describes the late festive meal or gathering.

## A table, but not one fixed menu

Some people prepare dinner at home; others choose a restaurant or a larger party. A quiet gathering can be just as much a réveillon as a lively evening with many guests. There is no universal menu, and the details depend on the people around the table. Conversation and the anticipation of midnight are often as important as the food.

## Wishes that continue after midnight

Bonne année ! means “Happy New Year!” and Meilleurs vœux ! means “Best wishes!” You can say these to friends, relatives or neighbours, or write them in a short message. In France, New Year wishes often continue through January, rather than belonging only to the first moments of the year. For a toast, À votre santé ! means “Cheers!” or, more literally, “To your health!”

## Recognise the custom, choose your words

Le gui means mistletoe. Some families or couples kiss beneath it at New Year, but not everyone follows this custom. For a little French practice, imagine three situations: greeting a neighbour, writing a card and raising a glass. Choose Bonne année !, Meilleurs vœux ! or À votre santé ! for each. Say your choices aloud, then check their meanings in the section above.
